Automated Induction Soldering Machine

I was asked to find out whether Opto 22 could replace an unreliable hot-air soldering process with induction heating. By the end of the internship, I had built and integrated a machine that fed terminals, placed them on a PCB, transferred the board, and ran it through a channel coil automatically.

01 · Assignment

I was handed an old machine and a new concept: induction soldering.

Opto 22 already had a machine that soldered SSR terminals onto PCBs using hot air, but it was old, complicated, and needed too much operator attention. There was also an induction-heating setup from Magneforce, an induction heating company, but it was only being used for stationary solder touch-up on the SSR. My job was to see whether induction could replace the old process and then build the automation around it.

I was told from the start that the project was ambitious for one summer and might not fully succeed. Because of that, I did not try to design the entire machine at once. I worked by proving one idea at a time and then building the next subsystem around what I learned.

02 · System Layout

The final machine was really two systems joined by one PCB handoff.

One side handled terminal feeding, pickup, datuming, flux, terminal placement, and PCB transfer. The other side handled continuous conveyor motion through the induction coil. The whole process only worked if the PCB could move from one half to the other without disturbing loose terminals.

A lot of the value of this project was in manufacturing and assembly.

The machine was built mainly from 80/20 aluminum extrusion, and I manufactured the custom brackets, plates, Delrin rails, fixtures, and many of the one-off parts on manual and CNC mills. I also used 3D printing wherever it made sense for speed or iteration.

03 · Why the Channel Coil

The original induction setup worked, but it was not a production process.

The existing Magneforce coil could solder all four terminals, but the PCB had to stay stationary inside the coil. That created two problems. First, it was awkward to turn into a continuous process. Second, the electromagnetic forces would disturb loose terminals during reflow, pulling the terminals out of tolerance.

KeepInduction heating as the soldering method
ChangeOpen the coil into a channel instead of a closed stationary setup
GainSlow continuous motion, better batch processing, less need to stop and start in the heating zone
Original stationary Magneforce induction setup used for rework/touch-up.
Core idea
Move the board through an open heating channel.

That one decision drove the rest of the machine. Once I committed to a channel coil, I needed a conveyor that could move the PCB smoothly through the field and a pick-and-place system that could hand the board off without upsetting the terminals.

05 · Coil Development

I hand-built three channel coils before settling on Mark 2.

I made the first coil from copper tubing and tested it right away. It was too long, had too much lead loss, and heated poorly. Mark 2 reduced both the lead lengths and the channel length, which improved the inductance and brought output up to about 1.37 kW from the 2.5 kW supply. Mark 3 added a second pass through the heating area, but it was harder to bend accurately and did not improve enough to justify the extra complexity.

Mark 1Proved the open-channel idea
Mark 2Best balance of performance and manufacturability
Mark 3More complexity with little payoff

We later commissioned Magneforce to build a purpose-made channel coil and sent them parts for testing, but the coil never arrived. That is why my Mark 2 coil became the final test coil and why the available hardware only soldered one terminal pair.

06 · Conveyor Development

I built a small test conveyor first because I needed to prove that induction soldering could work linearly.

The main question was simple: could a PCB with loose terminals move through the induction field and still solder correctly? I had never designed a conveyor before, so I built a tabletop prototype with chain, sprockets, shafts, a DC motor, and PCB pallets. It worked, which proved continuous linear induction soldering was possible.

From there I designed the final conveyor. I knew chain-mounted pallets would be hard to transfer PCBs onto without disturbing the terminals, so I separated support from drive. The PCB rode on adjustable Delrin rails while Delrin fingers attached to the chain pushed it along from behind.

PrototypeDirect chain pallets proved moving soldering
ProblemPallets were bad for transfer from another mechanism
Final solutionDelrin rails support the PCB while chain fingers only drive it

I also designed the drive system, tensioning, and a jack-screw transformer mount so the induction hardware could be aligned precisely even before the final coil geometry was known. Later the conveyor was upgraded from a DC motor to a servo motor with a 100:1 gearbox.

07 · Terminal Feeding

I replaced the old vibratory-bowl idea with a vertical feeder I called the PEZ dispenser.

The old machine used bowls and long tracks that could jam. I wanted the terminals stored in a simpler way and presented one complete set at a time. The result was a four-chute vertical feeder with an elevator that raised four terminals together. The final design held 400 terminals total, enough for 100 PCB cycles.

ChutesKeep the terminals from rotating into a bad orientation
Pickup headLofted geometry guides slightly misaligned terminals into the right pattern
Fine adjustmentLets the feeder be dialed in during integration instead of relying on perfect fabrication

The first version used resin parts, but friction was too high and the elevator was not strong enough. I moved to machined Delrin chutes, a PLA lofted pickup head, and a stronger three-piece aluminum elevator assembly.

08 · Terminal Gripper & Datum

I had to pick up four terminals with one parallel motion.

I used a pneumatic parallel gripper because it was the simplest reliable actuation method. The hard part was designing the custom geometry so one parallel motion could capture four separate terminals at once.

My first design put the locating geometry in the moving jaws. During integration I flipped that around: the pocketed center plate located the terminals, and the moving jaws only supplied the clamping force. That made pickup much more reliable. I also added a Z-datum station so the gripped terminals could be lowered onto a flat plate and all four bottoms would be set to the same height.

Flat jawsProvide clamping force only

09 · PCB Handling

The PCB transfer mattered because it connected everything.

I originally thought about another chain-based indexing system for the pick-and-place side, but chain positioning was not accurate enough for reliable placement. Instead, the PCB stayed supported on Delrin rails while a Y-axis mechanism moved it horizontally.

Two cylindrical locating features engaged the PCB’s existing semicircular cutouts, which gave the board a repeatable position. At the handoff point, the board was released directly into the conveyor pickup zone so the next Delrin finger could take over without any vertical drop or lift. I also offset the Y-axis gripper plate so it could retract far enough to clear the passing finger.

10 · Flux

I replaced the old flux sponge with a pumped weir cup to hold a constant level.

The old hot-air machine used a flux-filled sponge that needed constant operator attention. I wanted a cleaner and more repeatable way to apply flux, so I designed a dip cup with a weir wall. The idea was simple: low flow into the cup, overflow across the weir wall, and the liquid height is set by the weir wall itself.

In practice, fluid behavior fought me more than expected. Surface tension and restricted drainage meant the cup, tubing, and pump needed several revisions before the system worked consistently.

Why dip?Cleaner and more controlled than spraying the PCB
Why a weir wall?To hold a repeatable liquid height cycle to cycle

11 · Controls & Electrical

Automation and controls is where the machine came alive.

The controls work started because the conveyor was upgraded to a servo, so I learned how to control a StepperOnline A6 drive from an Opto 22 groov EPIC over Modbus RS-485, then expanded that approach until the machine had five servo drives: X, Y, Z, PEZ, and conveyor.

X, Y, Z, and PEZ used internal absolute positioning, while the conveyor ran continuously in speed mode. In PAC Control, which is Opto 22’s PLC software, I built reusable routines for setup, homing, motion, conveyor control, and feeder indexing. I first wired everything on plywood so I could iterate quickly, then later moved it into two Hoffman fiberglass enclosures for the final system.

MotionFour absolute axes plus one continuous conveyor axis
Hardwaregroov EPIC, A6 drives, relays, sensors, pneumatic outputs
Why it matteredThe machine only became real once all subsystems could run together

12 · Integration

The hardest problem was bringing all the individual subsystems together and integrating them.

Each subsystem could test well on its own and still fail once the whole sequence ran. Integration exposed pickup issues, feeder alignment error, flux-depth problems, taught-position changes, transfer timing, and mechanical interference.

Some of the most important design changes happened in this phase: I reversed the gripper locating scheme, used the feeder fine adjustment to dial in pickup, kept refining the flux setup, and tuned the motion sequence until the handoff and placement were reliable enough to run full cycles.

GripperChanged from locating jaws to a locating center plate
FeederFine-thread adjustment became critical during alignment
Project scopeLeft automatic PCB loading for later so the harder machine functions could be finished first

On the final day I ran four boards back-to-back from one Run Cycle command. Four cycles was just how many I had time to test. The feeder capacity and process logic were designed for 100 boards.

13 · Outcome

The machine proved the core idea: continuous induction soldering could be automated.

By the end of the internship, the machine could present four terminals, pick them, establish their Z datum, apply flux, place them on the PCB, transfer the board to the conveyor, and move it through the Mark 2 channel coil automatically.

The available induction hardware soldered two of the four terminals. A second setup intended for the other terminal size was never delivered, and there were still a few mechanical cleanup items left. Even with those limits, the main concept was proven.

What was proven

  • Continuous channel-coil induction soldering
  • Four-terminal storage, presentation, and pickup
  • Datum, flux, placement, and PCB transfer sequence
  • Rail-supported handoff into the soldering conveyor
  • Five-drive servo motion coordinated through PAC Control
  • Repeated automated cycles

What I would do next

  • Add the second induction setup for the other terminal pair
  • Finish transfer/clearance refinements
  • Add automatic PCB loading
  • Clean up the cabinet layout further
  • Run longer endurance testing

What I took from the project

The biggest thing I gained was experience building an entire machine instead of just one subsystem.
